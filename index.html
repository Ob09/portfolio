Featured · MSc Thesis

Predicting earthquake magnitude from 184 years of seismic records

Dundalk Institute of Technology · 2025 · Supervised by Dr Muhammad Adil Raja

My MSc thesis is the piece of work I'm proudest of, and it's where most of the skills on this page came together. The question was simple to ask but hard to answer: can a machine learning model, trained only on historical records, estimate how strong an earthquake will be — and explain why it gave that answer?

“Earthquakes are one of the most unpredictable natural disasters that cause loss of life and property within a span of a few seconds.”

From my thesis · Abstract

Nobody can predict exactly when or where an earthquake will hit. But magnitude can be estimated, and a good estimate helps emergency planners decide where resources, funding and mitigation should go first. That's the gap I set out to work on.

01 · Approach

How I built it

  • Pulled the historical catalogue from the USGS and the Associated Seismic Database — earthquakes across the US and parts of Canada — and cleaned it down to 44,231 reliable records.
  • Engineered new features: distance to the nearest city using the Haversine formula, log transforms for the heavily skewed depth and distance columns, and time-of-day categories.
  • Used Recursive Feature Elimination with a Random Forest to keep only the 10 predictors that actually carried signal.
  • Trained and compared seven models plus symbolic regression, then tuned the top two with RandomizedSearchCV.
02 · Findings

What the model learned

  • XGBoost came out on top, and across five cross-validation folds the RMSE stayed between 0.257 and 0.269 — so it wasn't a lucky split.
  • The linear models stayed below 0.55 R². The relationships in seismic data just aren't linear.
  • SHAP and LIME showed that epicentral distance, location, MMI and focal depth drove the predictions most.

“These findings not only validate the model statistically but also align perfectly with the theory of seismology”

From my thesis · Conclusion
03 · Deployment

Putting it to use

  • Deployed the tuned model behind a Flask REST API and tested it in Postman.
  • Built a Streamlit dashboard where anyone can enter earthquake parameters and get a prediction in real time.
  • Every prediction comes with its SHAP and LIME explanation, so the user sees why, not just what.
  • One thing I learned the hard way: the same tuned model scored 0.949 R² on Google Colab but 0.935 when retrained locally, purely because of library versions. I report the local number because that's the one I can reproduce.

Where it falls short, and what's next

The data only covers the US and nearby regions, and large earthquakes are rare in it, so I'd be careful using the model anywhere else. I was clear about that in the thesis too:

“The model evaluations should stand as a decision-support system but never as a formal predictor”

From my thesis · Discussion and Findings

If I took it further, I'd bring in global and more recent data, rebalance the rare large events with techniques like SMOTE, and test LSTM and transformer models against XGBoost.
